T Cells
• T cells have to be present at the site of interaction with 
antigen they do not secrete their receptors (vs. Ig) 
• Thymus: T cells mature (function)
• Importance of T cells: experiment- neonatal mice 
thymectomised- had wasting syndrome (chronic 
infections)
• Transplant of genetically-identical thymus- normal, could 
fight infection
NB: T cells only see antigenic determinants shown to 
them by special antigen presenting molecules on the 
surfaces of other cells and they do not recognize free 
antigen.

===== PAGE 11 =====
1. Cytotoxic T cells (CTL) kill virus-infected cells.
• Are identified by the surface marker CD8 (CD8+ T-cells)
• Control intracellular pathogens such as viruses and bacteria
• Require cell to cell contact to bind antigen
 
• Bind only antigen presented on the surface of cells
• 2 ways to kill infected cells: APOPTOSIS
• Engage the ‘death receptor’ Fas (CD95) on the target (CTLs 
bear the Fas ligand, CD95L), this activates apoptosis pathway
• Secrete the contents of “lytic granules” contain proteases called 
granzymes, and other proteins called perforins  that allow 
granzymes to enter into the target cell.
• http://www.hhmi.org/biointeractive/ctl-killing-target-cell
T-cell Subsets and Functions

===== PAGE 12 =====
2. Helper T cells (Th) provide “help” for cytotoxic T cells and B cells. 
• Are identified by the surface marker CD4 (CD4+ T-cells).
• Also require cell to cell contact to bind antigen.
• Bind only processed antigen
• Secrete cytokines and chemokines.

===== PAGE 14 =====
Memory T Cells
After a response to antigen:
•Rapid expansion of the relevant T cell clones (clonal 
expansion/proliferation) 
•The number of T cells quickly declines (effector cells 
function or effect their effect on infected cells and die)
•About 5% of the maximum amount are left a couple of 
weeks after infection (immune response against 
pathogen) or immunization. 
•These are memory cells, which have some of the 
attributes of stem cells, in that they replace themselves, 
as well as rapidly differentiating into effector cells when 
re-exposed to low antigen concentrations.

===== PAGE 21 =====
Human Leukocyte Antigens 
(HLA):
They are a group of proteins encoded within the major 
histocompatibility complex (MHC) on chromosome
6 in humans.
They are the proteins that the body uses to identify self.
They “Present” antigens for recognition by B- and T-cells.
There is variation between individuals and between ethnic
groups, the variation is quite extensive.

===== PAGE 24 =====
Human Leukocyte Antigens 
(HLA):
Each HLA allele encodes a surface protein that has
molecule has its own distinct requirements for peptide 
binding.
For example, HLA-A*0201 prefers to bind to leucine and 
valine, while HLA-A*0301 prefers to bind to leucine and 
lysine.
Therefore, a person’s constellation of HLA molecules
will determine which portions of a pathogen will be
presented to the immune system.
